Research

Black holes, tested against what we actually measure.

I work on gravitational-wave tests of general relativity, mostly by asking how much of a black hole's post-merger signal we can pin down from data alone, and where that stops matching the vacuum Kerr prediction.

Black hole spectroscopy & ringdown

After two black holes merge, the remnant relaxes to a stationary Kerr black hole by radiating a superposition of damped sinusoids — quasinormal modes (QNMs), each with a complex frequency set entirely by the remnant's mass and spin. Measuring two or more modes independently is a direct test of the no-hair property of Kerr black holes. My work is mostly on the data-analysis side of this: how many modes a given signal-to-noise ratio actually lets you resolve, how mode content depends on the progenitor binary's mass ratio and spins, and what systematics (start-time choice, overtones, nonlinear coupling) do to the inferred remnant parameters. I lead development on pyRing, the ringdown Bayesian-inference pipeline used by the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA Collaboration.

Quasinormal modes beyond linear theory

Linear QNM theory describes small perturbations of Kerr. Numerical-relativity waveforms show measurable departures from it even a few cycles after merger, including quadratic (nonlinear) modes sourced by the linear ones beating against themselves. I'm currently working on extracting and characterizing these quadratic modes from the SXS:BBH:3617 numerical-relativity waveform, matched to the parameters of GW250114, to see how much of the early post-merger signal they explain and what that implies for start-time systematics in spectroscopy analyses.

Tidal Love numbers

A Kerr black hole's tidal Love numbers vanish identically in general relativity — a sharp, parameter-free prediction, and a correspondingly sharp test if a compact object's Love numbers turn out to be nonzero. I lead a paper putting observational constraints on this: “No Love for black holes” (arXiv:2512.01918), currently under review at Physical Review D. See Publications for the full record.

Astrophysical environments

Real black holes don't merge in vacuum. I've worked on what surrounding matter and environmental effects do to gravitational-wave observables, and on using population-level constraints from GWTC catalog events to bound those effects — published in Physical Review Letters (Publications).
