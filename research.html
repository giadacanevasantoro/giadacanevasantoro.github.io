Research

How black holes settle down after they collide.

I use gravitational waves to test general relativity's predictions about black holes.

Listening to the ringdown

Just after two black holes merge, the new black hole isn't perfectly smooth. It wobbles and settles into its final shape, giving off a fading signal — a bit like a bell after it's struck. Each "note" in that signal is fixed entirely by the black hole's mass and spin. Detecting more than one note at once is a direct test of general relativity's prediction for what a black hole is. I work on how well we can actually pick these notes out of real data, and I help develop the analysis software used by the LIGO–Virgo–KAGRA gravitational-wave observatories.

Beyond the simple picture

The simplest description treats these wobbles as small. Real collisions are more violent than that, especially in the moments right after merger. I'm currently working out how much of that early signal comes from the wobbles interacting with each other, rather than something entirely new.

Can black holes be stretched?

General relativity predicts that an isolated black hole can't be deformed by nearby gravity at all — a sharp, testable claim. I led a paper putting this to the test against real observations. See Publications.

What's nearby matters

Real black holes don't merge in empty space. I've also studied what surrounding matter does to the gravitational-wave signal, and used data from many detected mergers together to constrain those effects.
